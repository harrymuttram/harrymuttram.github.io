About I study the political economy of police reform and how police behavior shapes what civilians believe about government. My work draws on body-worn camera footage-as-data, administrative records, and surveys. I am the Everyday Respect Predoctoral Fellow at USC's Security and Political Economy Lab and a Haynes Lindley Doctoral Dissertation Fellow. I am passionate about building and teaching quantitative methods for applied social science.

Does procedural justice training improve officer communication? Reevaluating the Oakland Police Department's body-worn camera footage

Muttram, Harry G. · PNAS Nexus

I reevaluate a procedural justice training conducted by the Oakland Police Department. Those trained earlier were already more procedurally just and made more stops; the reported treatment effect reflects these baseline differences in communication rather than the training itself.

Police as policymakers: how experiences with policy implementation shape policy representation

Sierra-Arévalo, Michael, et al. (incl. Harry G. Muttram) · Political Research Quarterly

We survey Los Angeles residents' views on policing to study policy as implemented by street-level bureaucrats: we find experiences and preferences are similar and statistically indistinguishable across racial groups, but general assessments of police are not. Thirty interviews suggest that shared preferences rest on different reasoning, and that the same police actions are read differently by race.

The subjectivity of respect in police traffic stops: modeling community perspectives in body-worn camera footage

Golazizian, Preni, et al. (incl. Harry G. Muttram) · Proceedings of ACL 64, pp. 33939–33961

Using respect ratings and written rationales of LAPD traffic stops from police-affiliated, justice-system-impacted, and non-affiliated Los Angeles residents, we train a model that conditions on annotator background. This model produces rationales closer to each group's own reasoning than other models.

American immigration attitudes and NIMBYism: do immigration preferences vary by spatial scale?

Lee, Jieun, and Harry G. Muttram (co-lead author) · PS: Political Science & Politics, 1–9

We conduct a conjoint experiment on a representative sample of U.S. adults to assess how much NIMBYism shapes immigration preferences. We find a small NIMBY effect, dwarfed by the attributes of the immigrant.

When are identities politically consequential? Identifying conditions of descriptive, substantive, and allied group identity

Bishin, Benjamin G., and Harry G. Muttram · The Forum 21: 339–357

We draw on decades of psychological research to specify the conditions under which identity should shape political behavior. We show that ignoring those conditions can produce mistaken conclusions about how identity groups vote in presidential elections.

Political control and policing: when do police comply with police reform?

Muttram, Harry G. (lead author), José J. Alcocer, Lauren Brown, Benjamin A.T. Graham, Michael Sierra-Arévalo, and Nicholas Weller · Journal of Policy Analysis and Management

We build and test a model of when officers comply with police reform, using a 2022 LAPD effort to reduce pretextual stops. Body-worn camera recordings show officers adopted the policy's verbal requirements and pretextual stops fell sharply, but interviews and stop data suggest that because sanctions never materialized, officers reverted to pre-policy behavior.

Community-informed AI models for police accountability

Everyday Respect Team (incl. Harry G. Muttram) · Humanities and Social Sciences Communications

We argue that AI models used to evaluate government accountability should incorporate diverse perspectives rather than a single ground truth. We document our approach to community-informed model training, in which annotators are recruited from the populations whose judgments the model is meant to represent.

Working with majority peers stifles the behavioral benefits of police diversification: evidence from Los Angeles

Muttram, Harry G.

I test academic objections to the efficacy of police diversification for reducing aggressive policing. Comparing the same officer across analogous working conditions, I show that Black and female officers are significantly more likely to search and less likely to arrest when working with majority-group peers.

Police subject Black and Hispanic drivers to more intensive traffic stops that are perceived as less legitimate

Muttram, Harry G., with Benjamin A.T. Graham, Michael Sierra-Arévalo, and Nicholas Weller

We use body-worn camera annotations to build a latent measure of stop intensity, capturing the cumulative intrusiveness of officer actions during a stop. We show that this measure varies by driver race and predicts legitimacy as assessed by independent annotators.

Family in blue: the effects of familial exposure to employment by the punitive hand of the government

Muttram, Harry G., with Marcel Roman, Benjamin Newman, and Tyler Reny

We build on work assessing the liberalizing effects of carceral contact by testing whether police family members exert the opposite, conservatizing effect. Familial contact generates pro-police orientations and "law and order" views across racial lines, but race bounds these effects: among non-Whites, it neither conservatizes race-related views nor erodes support for police reform.

Does race predict civilian escalation? Testing a mechanism underlying bias in officer-involved shootings

Muttram, Harry G., with Benjamin A.T. Graham, Michael Sierra-Arévalo, and Nicholas Weller

We test an implication of a formal model of police bias, that White civilians should escalate more, using a novel measure of civilian escalation drawn from body-worn camera footage. We find no evidence that they do.

Simulating Confounders for Sensitivity Analysis in Generalized Linear and Additive Models

Muttram, Harry G., with David Armstrong

Existing tools for gauging how much an omitted variable would change a result work only for linear models. We propose a simulation procedure that extends this reasoning to nonlinear models by inserting a confounder of known strength; an accompanying R package is in development.

Software

Shiny app · R
Should we trust your policy evaluation?
This is a simulation tool I built for building intuition about the efficacy of causal inference estimators. The idea is that you can set the true policy effect in a hypothetical impact evaluation, add complications like confounding or autocorrelation, and see how pre-post, two-way fixed effects, and regression discontinuity in time methods perform across a bunch of simulated datasets. Feel free to try it out or share with your students.
